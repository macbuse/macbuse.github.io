### **The Visual Evolution**

If you look at the ancient version of this character (from thousands of years ago), it looked like a slab of meat with **ribs** or **muscle fibers** showing.

* **The Outer Frame:** This represents the boundary or the skin of the cut.
* **The "Inside" (the two 人 shapes):** These aren't actually "people" (*rén*), even though they look like it! They are a stylized way of drawing the **muscle grain** or the **rib cage** inside the meat.

> **Visual Trick:** Imagine a **rack of ribs** hanging in a shop window. The two marks inside are the layers of meat and bone.

---

### **The "Meat" Radical (⺼)**

Here is the really clever part. Because the character **肉** is a bit "fat" and takes up too much space, when it is used *inside* other words (as a building block called a radical), it gets squished.

It turns into **⺼**, which looks almost exactly like the word for "Moon" (**月**).

**Because of this, most Chinese words related to the body contain this "meat" block:**

* **Shoulder:** 肩 (*Jiān*) — See the **⺼** at the bottom?
* **Stomach:** 胃 (*Wèi*) — See the **⺼** at the bottom?
* **Legs:** 腿 (*Tuǐ*) — The **⺼** is on the left! (Relevant for your **tuǐ téng** / sore legs!)

### **1. Describing the Situation**

To state clearly that you have two friends who are seriously ill in the hospital, you can structure the sentence like this:

> **"Wǒ yǒu liǎng gè péngyou dèngbìng zhùyuàn le, qíngkuàng hěn yánzhòng."**
> *(Wuo yo lyang guh pung-yo jong-beeng joo-ywan luh, cheeng-kwahng hun yan-jong.)*
> **"I have two friends [who are] seriously ill and hospitalized, the situation is very severe."**

* **Liǎng gè (两个):** The counting word for "two" items/people.
* **Zhùyuàn (住院):** Literally "to reside in the hospital." This single word replaces the need to say "in the hospital."
* **Qíngkuàng (情况):** Situation / condition.

---

### **2. Talking about Visiting or Checking on Them**
